Chapter 004 · Lesson 3

The order inside a day, and what a Payment pays

Meera pays her chain on the 10th, the same day its interest is added. Does her Payment come before the interest or after it? Which Principal is next month charged on? And when she pays more than the interest, where does the rest go? One loop, accrue_through, answers all three.

By the end you can hand-trace a run of Payments in the engine's order, including one made on an Accrual Date and one made mid-month.

The problem

Take the obvious design: on each Accrual Date, charge the rate on whatever the Principal is that day. Then Meera could pay ₹50,000 of Principal on 9 Oct, and on 10 Oct be charged on ₹30,000 for a month in which she'd had ₹80,000 for 29 days. Pay it on the 9th, borrow it again on the 11th, and she has dodged almost a whole month's interest. The app's first design did exactly this (ADR 0006).

The order on one day matters just as much. If her Payment on the 10th came before the 10th's interest, it couldn't pay that interest. Under Compounded Monthly, the interest would then be capitalised that same day, even though she'd brought the money in.

The idea

Each month is charged on the Principal as it stood at the start of that month, and a month starts at the very start of its Accrual Date, before that day's Payments. On each Accrual Date the engine does, in this order:

  1. apply every Payment dated before today;
  2. add the month just ended's interest, on that month's starting Principal, less what Payments already paid ahead;
  3. start the new month: note today's Principal as its starting Principal;
  4. apply today's Payments;
  5. capitalise what's still unpaid, if the Interest Mode says so (lesson 4).

Each Payment pays, in order: unpaid interest, then the Interest to Date (paid ahead of the month's interest), then Principal.

The flow

Notice that step 3 comes before step 4. Today's Payments belong to the month that has just started, like any Payment later in that month.

The code

1. The loop

  1. := is Python's "walrus" operator: it assigns on and tests it in one expression. The loop runs once per Accrual Date up to day. self.n is the number of the next one.
  2. The month is charged on month_start_principal, not on self.principal. A Payment of Principal during the month changes self.principal but not this.
  3. Anything Payments already paid ahead of this month's interest is taken off, so it isn't charged twice.
  4. The new month begins, and only after that are today's Payments applied (line 282). This is the line the check-yourself exercise moves.

2. What a Payment pays

  1. All unpaid interest first, oldest and newest alike: the engine keeps one running total.
  2. Then the Interest to Date: the month under way so far, today included (lesson 2). It isn't owed yet in the Ledger, so paying it is paying ahead. paid_ahead remembers it, and line 266 counts it against the month's interest when it's charged.
  3. Only what's left reaches the Principal. There's no way to pay next month's interest early beyond the Interest to Date: the rest is Principal.

3. The guard: pay_through and Overpayment

self.pending is the sorted list from lesson 1. Each Payment is taken off the front once its date is reached. A Payment of more than the whole Outstanding on its date raises Overpayment (defined at line 93), and chapter 003's check_replays turns that into a 422.

4. After the loop: the Interest to Date as a charge

  1. Also on the month's starting Principal: the same rule as the month's interest.
  2. The Ledger's last step adds the Interest to Date into unpaid interest, less what was paid ahead. It's a live figure: tomorrow's replay gives a bigger one, and on the next Accrual Date it gives way to the month's interest (ADR 0011).

Nuances

Each one changes one thing about the chain. All figures are from scripts/run_throughs.py (section L3).

(a) Paying Principal the day before an Accrual Date

Predict: suppose Meera paid ₹50,000 on 9 Oct. What is the 10 Oct interest: ₹1,600, or 2% of what's left?

₹1,600. The month from 10 Sep began on ₹80,000, so it's charged on ₹80,000. On 9 Oct the Interest to Date was 30 days' worth, the whole ₹1,600, and her Payment paid it ahead. So 10 Oct's interest is all paid and nothing is unpaid. The ₹47,200 of Principal she paid lowers interest from the next month: 10 Nov is charged on ₹32,800. The trick from "The problem" no longer works. The test test_paying_principal_the_day_before_an_accrual_date_does_not_skip_that_months_interest pins it.

(b) Paying on the Accrual Date itself

Predict: now she pays the same ₹50,000 on 10 Oct instead, a day later. Which Principal is the month from 10 Oct to 10 Nov charged on?

On ₹80,000. The month from 10 Oct began at the start of 10 Oct, before her Payment (step 3 before step 4). Her Payment came after the 10 Oct interest, so it could pay it: all ₹2,800 unpaid. Then it paid 1 day of the new month ahead (₹53, since today always counts), and the rest went to Principal. That Principal lowers interest from the month after: 10 Dec. On 10 Nov the chain's first anniversary then capitalises the ₹1,547 still unpaid (lesson 4).

So a Payment on the 10th is treated like one on the 11th or the 25th: it belongs to the month starting that day. Test: test_a_payment_on_an_accrual_date_lowers_interest_from_the_next_month.

(c) A mid-month Payment bigger than the interest

₹3,267. The Ledger still ends with the ₹533 Interest to Date step, but it's marked all paid ahead, so it adds nothing. Outstanding is now ₹76,733. Next month is still charged on ₹80,000, because this month began on ₹80,000.

(d) You can't pay next month's interest early

Say she pays exactly ₹3,333 on 19 Oct: ₹2,800 unpaid plus ₹533 ahead, nothing to Principal. On 10 Nov, November's ₹1,600 is charged, and her ₹533 counts against it, leaving ₹1,067 unpaid (which that day's anniversary then capitalises: lesson 4). A Payment pays ahead only as far as the Interest to Date on its own date. In (c), the extra ₹3,267 went to Principal, not to November. Test: test_a_payment_cannot_cover_interest_not_yet_charged.

(e) Capitalised is not covered

If she pays nothing more, on 10 Nov her unpaid interest is capitalised (lesson 4) and drops to ₹0. Yet months_unpaid says 3, up from 2 on 9 Nov: September's remainder, October and November. Moving interest into Principal doesn't pay it. Test: test_capitalised_interest_still_counts_as_unpaid.

(f) The Payment of exactly the Outstanding, and one rupee more

₹83,334 on 19 Oct raises Overpayment: Payment of 8333400 paise on 2026-10-19 exceeds the 8333300 paise owed. Exactly ₹83,333 is allowed, and the engine applies it as an ordinary Payment that leaves nothing owed. In the app, though, saving it does more.

Trap #3, a warning: paying exactly the Outstanding Redeems the Mortgage, in the same request. That happens in record_payment, not in the engine. Chapter 005, lesson 3 owns it; see the traps sheet.
Newcomer trap #7: the month starts before the day's Payments. A month is charged on the Principal at its start. A Payment on an Accrual Date comes after that day's interest, and belongs to the new month. Today always counts in the Interest to Date. So paying Principal never lowers the month it's paid in. This chapter owns trap #7; see the traps sheet.

Quiz

₹1,00,000 at 3%, full month, from 14 Jan. On 14 Feb, after that day's ₹3,000 interest, ₹53,000 is paid: ₹3,000 unpaid, ₹3,000 ahead, ₹47,000 Principal. What is the 14 Mar interest?

The month from 14 Feb began before the Payment, on ₹1,00,000, so 14 Mar charges ₹3,000. The Payment paid it all ahead (full month: the whole month from day 1), so none of it is unpaid, but the charge is still ₹3,000, not ₹0. ₹1,590 comes on 14 Apr. This is the test the exercise breaks.

Unpaid ₹1,200, Interest to Date ₹480, Principal ₹80,000. A Payment of ₹2,000 pays how much Principal?

Unpaid first (₹1,200), then the Interest to Date (₹480, paid ahead), then Principal: ₹2,000 − ₹1,680 = ₹320.

Which steps does months_unpaid read?

It queues each month's charge and lets Payments cover them oldest first. The Interest to Date never counts as a month, and Capitalisation doesn't cover one.
