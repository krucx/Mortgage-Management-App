Lesson 2 · The Ledger engine

One day inside the replay

Most of the engine's rules come down to one question: on an Accrual Date, what happens first? Get the order right and you can predict every interest line and every Capitalisation by hand.

By the end: you can say the order of events on an Accrual Date, and use it to predict which Principal a month's interest is charged on.

The order on an Accrual Date

This is accrue_through (ledger.py:260), trimmed to its steps. It runs once for each Accrual Date up to the date being replayed to:

while (on := accrual_date(start, n)) <= day:
    month_terms = terms_from(previous Accrual Date)   # the month just ending
    ① pay_through(on - 1 day)                         # Payments before today
    ② interest = monthly_interest(month_start_principal, month_terms)
       unpaid += interest
    ③ month_start_principal = principal               # next month starts NOW,
                                                      # before today's Payments
    ④ pay_through(on)                                 # today's Payments
    ⑤ if month_terms capitalise: principal += unpaid  # what's still unpaid
    ⑥ if Terms change from today: record term_changed step

Two rules come out of this. Everything else in this lesson follows from them.

Rule A. A month's interest is charged on the Principal as it stood when that month began (step ②). It does not matter what happened to the Principal during the month (ADR 0006).

Rule B. On an Accrual Date the order is interest, then new month, then that day's Payments, then Capitalisation (②③④⑤). So a Payment that day does stop that day's interest from compounding, but it does not lower the Principal for the month that has just started.

To keep the numbers clean, every example below uses the nothing Part-Month Rule, so no Interest to Date appears. That is lesson 3. All figures come from running the real compute_ledger.

Rule A: paying Principal mid-month

Lesson 1 left a puzzle. Principal fell to ₹50,733 on 20 Jan, yet February's interest was still ₹2,000. Rule A explains it. February's interest is for the month that began on 10 Jan, when the Principal was ₹1,00,000. The lower Principal only counts from the month that began on 10 Feb.

ADR 0006 gives the reason for this rule. If interest were charged on the Principal at the Accrual Date, a Customer could pay off Principal the day before and avoid almost a whole month's interest. The rule matches the lending habit of "a month started is a month charged". The other option, weighting interest by days, was rejected because it turns every interest line into a calculation with several parts.

Rule B: one day makes a month's difference

The month from 10 Mar to 10 Apr started at step ③, before the Payment at step ④. So it began with ₹1,00,000 of Principal and is charged ₹2,000. Paying one day later cost the Customer a whole month at the old Principal. The Payment also had to clear that morning's ₹2,000 of interest first, so ₹2,000 less of it reached Principal.

This is deliberate. ADR 0006 records that until ADR 0011, a Payment on an Accrual Date counted for the month starting that day. That was changed so that paying off the Principal on an Accrual Date cannot let the Customer off the month that has just begun.

Rule B again: Capitalisation

The same loan, but compounded monthly. The Customer pays the ₹2,000 of interest either on the Accrual Date or a day late:

10 Febnothing unpaid, no Capitalisation1,00,0002,0001,02,000
11 Feb2,000 capitalised1,02,000 → 1,00,000 on the 11th2,040 on 1,02,0001,02,040

In the first row, step ④ (the Payment) comes before step ⑤ (Capitalisation), so the interest is paid before it can compound. In the second row it has already been capitalised by the 11th, so the whole Payment goes to Principal. Rule A then means March is still charged on ₹1,02,000.

Two smaller rules from the same loop

Term Changes. month_terms is read from the previous Accrual Date. So a Term Change effective 10 Apr does not affect the interest charged on 10 Apr, or whether that day capitalises. It applies from the month that begins on 10 Apr (glossary: "it never rewrites earlier interest").

Month-end. accrual_date(start, n) (ledger.py:106) always counts from the start date and clamps to the end of the month. A loan started on 31 Jan accrues on 28 Feb, then 31 Mar, then 30 Apr. It never slips to the 28th for good.

Check yourself

1. What is the order of events on an Accrual Date when a Payment is made that day?

Steps ②③④⑤. Interest is charged for the month ending, the new month opens on the Principal before any Payments, then the day's Payments are applied, then whatever is still unpaid is capitalised.

2. Simple interest, ₹1,00,000 at 2%, Accrual Date the 10th, nothing unpaid. The Customer pays ₹50,000 of Principal on 25 Apr. What is 10 May's interest?

This is Rule A. The month ending on 10 May began on 10 Apr with ₹1,00,000. The ₹50,000 counts from the month that begins on 10 May, so 10 Jun's interest is ₹1,000.

3. Compounded monthly: why does paying the interest on the Accrual Date stop it compounding?

Step ④ comes before step ⑤. Only what is still unpaid after that day's Payments is moved into Principal.

4. A Term Change from 2% to 1.5% takes effect on 10 Apr. Which rate charges the interest on 10 Apr?

The interest on 10 Apr is for the month that began on 10 Mar, and month_terms is read from that date. The new rate first appears in the interest charged on 10 May.

5. (From lesson 1) Case 1's ₹52,000 on 9 Mar is first entered on 20 May. What happens to the stored April interest line?

This was a trick question. No interest line is stored. The next replay includes the 9 Mar Payment and charges April ₹1,000, on ₹50,000.
