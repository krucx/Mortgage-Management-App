Lesson 1 · The Ledger engine

The Ledger is a replay

There is no ledger table. Every interest line, every Payment split and every Outstanding is replayed from a handful of stored facts each time something asks for it. That one choice is why a back-dated Payment is easy to handle.

By the end: you can say exactly what is stored, what is derived, and why adding a Payment dated three months ago rewrites later lines that nobody edited.

What is stored, and what isn't

Here is everything a Replay reads from the database (replay.py:278, two queries for any number of Mortgages):

mortgage
start date, amount lent,
Terms, Closure
term_change
effective_on, new Terms
allocation ⋈ payment
mortgage, amount, paid_on
where voided_at is null
→
compute_ledger()
→
Ledger steps → Outstanding,
Months Unpaid, Receipts…

Solid boxes are stored. Dashed boxes exist only in memory.

The thing to notice: an Allocation stores only how much of a Payment went to a Mortgage. It does not store how much of it was interest and how much was Principal. That split is worked out by the replay every time, from everything that came before it (payments.py:138 says so in a comment).

How a replay runs

compute_ledger (ledger.py:339) is a pure function, with no database access. It starts on the start date with the amount lent as Principal, then walks forward one Accrual Date at a time. On each one it applies the Payments made before that date, charges the month's interest, applies that day's Payments, and capitalises if the Terms say so. It finishes with the Interest to Date for the month under way. Each action appends one immutable LedgerStep, which records the running Principal and unpaid interest after it.

The exact order inside a day is lesson 2. For now, all you need is that the replay walks forward from the start, and only the stored facts above go into it.

Every line after 20 Jan is different. The 12 Mar Payment, which nobody touched, now pays ₹1,617 of Principal, because there was less interest waiting for it. Outstanding fell from ₹1,02,400 to ₹50,226.

Nothing had to find and fix those rows, because they were never stored. The next request replays the facts, and the new fact is now one of them.

Two puzzles to bring to lesson 2: why is February's interest still ₹2,000 when the Principal fell on 20 Jan, and why did the 20 Jan Payment pay ₹733 of interest when nothing was due yet? (ADR 0006 and ADR 0011.)

The general name for this is a retroactive event. Martin Fowler describes finding the "branch point" where the right and wrong histories split, then replaying forward from it (Fowler, "Retroactive Event"). This app takes the simplest version: the branch point is always the start date, because replaying a few hundred Mortgages takes milliseconds (ADR 0001).

Refusing a bad change

The cost of replaying is that a back-dated fact can make history impossible. Suppose the Payment on 20 Jan were ₹2,00,000. Only ₹1,00,733 was owed that day. So every change follows the same three steps in one transaction (changes.py:184):

with engine.begin() as conn:            # one transaction
    write the Payment / Void / Term Change / Closure
    check_replays(conn, rows, today)    # replay each touched Mortgage
        # compute_ledger raises Overpayment if any Allocation
        # is more than was owed on its date
        # → 422, and the transaction rolls back

The engine refuses: "the Payment on 20 Jan 2026 would be more than the ₹1,00,733 owed then". This applies to every kind of change. Voiding an earlier Payment or adding a Term Change can make a later Payment an overpayment, and the same check catches it.

Voids follow the same idea. A Voided Payment stays in the payment table with voided_at and a reason, and the replay's query leaves it out (replay.py:292). Nothing is deleted, and no reversing entry is needed.

Isn't replaying everything slow?

Within one request, MortgageReplay keeps each Ledger it builds, keyed by date (replay.py:112). Across requests nothing is cached, so nothing can go stale. The original spec had a startup "catch-up" job that wrote interest into a stored table. That job no longer exists.

Check yourself

1. Where is the interest/Principal split of a recorded Payment stored?

An Allocation is just (payment, mortgage, amount). The split comes from the payment step the replay makes, which is why it can change when an earlier fact arrives.

2. A Payment dated 20 Jan is recorded on 15 Apr. What updates March's interest line?

March's interest line is not stored anywhere. The next time any screen asks, the replay runs over the new set of facts and charges March on the lower Principal.

3. How is a Payment larger than what was owed on its date refused?

check_replays replays inside the same engine.begin() transaction. Overpayment becomes a 422, and leaving the block with an exception rolls the write back.

4. How does Voiding a Payment remove it from the Ledger?

The query in replays() has voided_at IS NULL. The Payment and its reason stay on record for History. The Ledger simply never sees it.

5. Why did ADR 0001 reject a stored MortgageLedger table?

A back-dated Payment, a corrected amount or a rate edit would each need every later row rewritten. A pure replay can't drift, because it has no copy that could fall out of date.
